## Sections
1. **I. GRANULARITY OF LOCKS:** The paper frames lock size as a tradeoff: fine locks improve concurrency for small transactions but increase lock-management and storage costs for large ones. It proposes letting multiple lock sizes coexist so workloads can choose an appropriate point on that tradeoff.
2. **Hierarchical locks:** In a tree of resources, shared and exclusive locks cover descendants implicitly, while intention locks mark ancestors and prevent conflicting coarse locks. The section develops intention, intention-share, intention-exclusive, and share-and-intention-exclusive modes, and explains how they let transactions safely combine coarse scans and fine updates.
3. **Access modes and compatibility:** The six modes (including no lock) express distinct permissions and are paired with a compatibility matrix. Their semantics allow compatible readers and compatible intentions to proceed while excluding combinations that could conflict below the node.
4. **Rules for requesting nodes:** A transaction must acquire the appropriate intention locks from the root toward a requested node, and release locks from leaves toward roots unless it holds them to transaction end. These rules preserve the meaning of implicit subtree locks as the transaction navigates the hierarchy.
5. **Several examples:** The examples show how to read or update one record, scan a file while occasionally updating it, and quiesce the database. They illustrate that SIX can permit a scan alongside readers without paying to lock every record individually.
6. **Directed acyclic graphs of locks:** The model generalizes from trees to DAGs, such as structures where files, indexes, and records have multiple logical or physical access paths. Shared access can be implied through a parent path, but exclusive access requires protecting every parent path; the examples distinguish operations that need only one path from updates that must secure all ancestors.
7. **Proof of equivalence of the lock protocol:** The authors define a lock graph and its projection onto atomic resources, then prove that compatible explicit lock graphs yield compatible implicit locks on the leaves. This establishes that the multi-mode, multi-granularity scheme does not admit conflicts hidden by its implicit locking.
8. **Dynamic lock graphs:** Index intervals provide lockable predicates for ranges of keys and help protect against phantoms. When graph nodes move as records are inserted, deleted, or updated, the protocol requires exclusive protection in both old and new positions and forbids creating cycles.
9. **Scheduling and granting requests:** Fair queues group compatible requests and grant the compatible head group, with a group mode representing their combined permissions. The discussion explains how new requests join, how releases can make a waiting group eligible, and why waiting conversions receive priority over new requests.
10. **Conversions:** A repeated request is combined with the transaction’s current mode using the privilege ordering; it can be granted immediately when compatible with other holders or must wait otherwise. Conflicting conversions can deadlock, so conversion scheduling must account for the granted group and detect cycles.
11. **Deadlock and lock thrashing:** The wait-for relation is derived from incompatible granted or queued requests, and a cycle means some requests must be preempted with transaction changes undone. The paper also argues that local deadlock handling cannot prevent overload-driven lock thrashing by itself; a higher-level scheduler must regulate transaction admission and progress.
12. **II. DEGREES OF CONSISTENCY:** Transactions preserve database assertions by grouping actions that may temporarily make data inconsistent, and recovery undoes a failed transaction. In concurrent execution, degrees 0 through 3 describe progressively stronger guarantees about dirty writes, commit timing, dirty reads, and repeatable reads.
13. **Informal definition of consistency:** Degree 0 prevents overwriting another transaction’s dirty data; degree 1 also defers commits to transaction end; degree 2 additionally avoids dirty reads; degree 3 also prevents other transactions from changing data already read. The gauge, summary, and display example shows why different operations may choose different degrees and how weaker degrees affect recoverability and consistency of derived outputs.
14. **Lock protocol definition of consistency:** The paper maps each degree to lock duration and mode: long exclusive write locks are central to degrees 1–3, while reads gain short shared locks at degree 2 and long shared locks at degree 3. It then restates the guarantees using well-formed and two-phase transactions, distinguishing consistency requirements from the stronger practice of holding every lock to transaction end.
15. **Consistency of schedules:** A schedule records the ordered actions of concurrent transactions, making committed and dirty values explicit. The authors define legal schedules and consistency by applying the earlier degree definitions to each transaction’s observed history.
16. **Dependencies among transactions:** Three dependency relations capture write-write, write-read, and read-write orderings at increasing consistency degrees. A schedule is consistent at a degree exactly when the corresponding transitive dependency relation is a partial order, connecting transaction observations to a serial ordering and exposing anomalies that lower degrees permit.
17. **Relationship to transaction backup and recovery:** Degree 1 prevents recovery of an incomplete transaction from erasing completed updates; degree 2 also prevents reads of data that recovery may undo; and degree 3 makes a transaction’s outputs repeatable under the stated conditions. The section traces undo/redo recovery from checkpoints and explains how the guarantees strengthen as all transactions use higher degrees.
18. **Cost of degrees of consistency:** Lower degrees can reduce lock storage and calls, while stronger read isolation increases retained shared locks and waiting. The paper gives explicitly crude estimates and a banking example, concluding that degrees 2 and 3 have similar baseline lock costs, with actual waiting costs highly sensitive to contention.
19. **III. LOCK GRANULARITY AND DEGREES OF CONSISTENCY IN EXISTING SYSTEMS:** IMS/VS and DMS 1100 are compared against the proposed modes, granularities, and consistency degrees. The discussion distinguishes recognizing intention modes from actually exploiting implicit locks, describes each system’s available guarantees and user controls, and presents the IBM research system’s dynamic DAG, interval locks, and transaction-selectable degrees.
20. **ACKNOWLEDGMENT:** The authors credit colleagues for discussions about existing locking systems and improving the presentation. The acknowledgment identifies practical design feedback behind the comparison and exposition.
21. **REFERENCES:** Three references point to the consistency and predicate-lock work, the IMS/VS application design guide, and the DMS 1100 documentation. They place the paper’s formal background and system descriptions in their source literature.
## What it means for Concord v3
The paper’s database locks protect resources according to explicit compatibility, hierarchy, and transaction-lifetime rules; this supports treating lock semantics as deliberate coordination policy in the v3 architecture’s **claims for presence** and **server-side changes that land** sections. Its multi-granularity scheme is not evidence that all locks are harmful: it trades concurrency against lock-management overhead and applies to database resources, so it does not support the draft’s reported claim that Cursor found file locks harmful; the supplied context says Cursor’s locks were agent-managed locks on TASKS in a shared coordination file. The paper’s deadlock and lock-thrashing discussion also suggests that any v3 section on **issue dependencies and the ready set** should distinguish dependency scheduling from resource locking and account for admission/progress policy where lock contention exists.
